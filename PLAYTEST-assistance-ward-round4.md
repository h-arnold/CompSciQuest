- Player's goal and information available: learn the four taught verbs and see the result of PLACE/PUT. The room text names the block and the chalk outline inside the cabinet, so the placement is meant to be observable. No score or progress depends on the block, so the ward can still be finished.
- Why it is a problem: the closure claim says `look` should stop describing the block as sitting on top and the block should remain discoverable. - Why the next step was unclear and whether it changed: the next attempts changed from taking the block out to trying other phrasings, then to examining the cabinet and moving on; none changed the room text.
- Evidence: steps 42–53 and C9–C13; screenshots `playtest-round4-3-demonstration-placed-block.png` and `playtest-round4-4-place-block-not-discoverable.png`. Route: `content-editor` (state-aware room prose).

## 6. Difficulty and fairness record

**Area 1 — goal, obstacle and available tools.** All three of Bycer's questions were answerable without notes: the register and AI verdict give the goal (“The entry stands until mastery of the whole of Unit 1 is demonstrated. You will demonstrate it here.”), the recovery board and blocked dial give the five-part obstacle (“All five records are required.”), and the four pupils, practice props and AI audit are visible tools. Every gate had prose plus a physical label or plaque.

**Area 2 — clue discoverability and repeated progress.** Time to first meaningful insight: from New Game (12:39) to Nia's chipped corner (about 12:41) took roughly two minutes in this session, most of it ordinary exploration. The final ward puzzle was understood when the board's five labels were read and solved when the dial confirmed all five sockets lit. Repeated actions with no new information: `examine recovery board` repeated the five labels after each recovery; the dial block repeated its socket list twice; `hint` during cooldown repeated “Think about it for a moment.”; the eight attempts to find the placed block produced no new information and are finding 1. Both N6 clues were found for each gate through ordinary exploration.

**Area 3 — reasonable commands rather than one exact phrase.** Spaced `talk to`, hyphenated `talk-to`, written `PLACE`, `put`, both item-first unlock forms, Priya's own-words reason and all four arrival EXAMINE synonyms worked. The wrong-order unlock and the fixed bell/cabinet produced in-world reasons rather than bare refusals. All three taught quiz answer forms worked, so no essential action depended on one exact phrase. The only avoidable wording friction this round was the unfindable placed block (finding 1).

**Area 4 — hints.** Level 1 in each of the three ward rooms, level 2 and 3 in Demonstration when pressed, and the cooldown line on immediate repeats. Hints named the stalled object or half-step without supplying an answer, cost no score and were never offered automatically. There was no automatic offer before genuine stuck evidence; the ladder was available on request and the cooldown prevented rapid rung-dumping. After a hint the next action was clear (for example, “read it and fit the key to that step yourself.”).

**Area 5 — quiz readability, consequences and explanations.** Four questions were shown with numbered options in a dedicated panel; the three-of-four pass, zero score, no life loss, no movement and unlimited retries were stated before question 1. Every answer, right or wrong, got a specific reason including why the distractors were wrong. The deliberate 2/4 fail left the pupil in Records at score 0, lives 3 with an immediate retry; the retry began at “Question 1 of 4”. The unlock explanation named both working forms, so a correct answer could be copied into the room. A wrong answer changed the next attempt from guessing to using the explanation.

**Area 6 — failure preservation, repeated progress and GCSE suitability.** No ward failure removed a record or a life, moved the pupil or forced a repeated walk. Ward lives stayed at 3 and score at 0 from New Game to completion. The reasoning involves observation, evidence, command form and a five-part sequence, which suits a GCSE learner; difficulty comes from interpretation rather than dense prose or punishment. After a hint or failure the player could say what to try next and do it: the dial named “ORIENTATION PASS needs the practice quiz”, the quiz explanations named the rules, Priya asked for the reason in the pupil's own words, and the room hints named the stalled step. Abandonment point: none in the ward. The point most likely to cause doubt was the repeated refusal when trying to find the placed block (finding 1); it was resolved by moving on because the block is optional.
