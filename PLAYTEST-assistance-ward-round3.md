## 5. Findings from this round

### Functional defect 1. The taught “exact option text” quiz answer form does not work

- Command or click: during live quiz questions, `download, then import` (option 4 shown verbatim), `unlock <item> <target>` (option 3 shown verbatim), `answer N; a bare N; the exact option text` (option 2 shown verbatim) and `open <target>` (option 1 shown verbatim).
- Observed: `> download, then import` / “I don't understand that.”; `> unlock <item> <target>` / “I don't understand that.”; `> answer N; a bare N; the exact option text` / “That answer is not one of the choices.”; `> open <target>` / “I don't understand that.” The quiz panel stayed on the same question after each. `answer N` and bare `N` both worked (“The second option is correct…”).
- Why it is a problem: the AI explicitly teaches “A bare N or the exact option text is also accepted” and the quiz explanation repeats it, but a pupil who copies an option verbatim is met with a parser refusal or a false “not one of the choices”. This is the named answer-form behaviour, not a matter of taste: one of the three advertised forms does not work.
- Evidence: steps 113, 115, 117, 89 and 112; screenshot 6 (live quiz and framed answer rules). Route: `code-lead`.

### Content-quality finding 1. Demonstration prose keeps the practice block on top after it is put away

- Command or click: in the clean New Game tab, `take practice block`, then `put practice block in practice cabinet` (“You put it down.”), then `look`.
- Observed: “A practice cabinet squats beside the instruction card, a practice block on top and a practice key hanging from its handle.” The block is still described as on top after the successful PUT. In the main tab, four natural attempts to take the block back (`take practice block`, `take block from practice cabinet`, `take the practice block from the cabinet`, `look in practice cabinet`) returned “I don't understand that.” or a plain room description.
- Player's goal and information available: learn the four taught verbs and check the result of PLACE/PUT. The cabinet description mentions “a chalk outline inside shows where the practice block belongs”, so the placement is meant to be visible.
- Why it is a problem: the room prose contradicts the successful action at the moment the ward is teaching observation. It is recoverable because the block is optional and the action succeeded, but a pupil may repeat the placement or assume it failed.
- Hint level used: none; the Demonstration level-1 hint correctly names the cabinet's separate lock and door, so no hint repairs a false room description.
- Why the next step was unclear and whether it changed: the next attempts changed from taking the block out to trying other phrasings, and then to asking Nia and Mateo instead; none changed the room text.
- Evidence: steps 64–66, 71–75, 133 and 148; screenshot 11. Route: `content-editor` (state-aware room prose).

## 6. Difficulty and fairness record

**Goals, obstacle and tools (Bycer's three questions).** All three were answerable without external notes. The register and AI verdict state the goal (“The entry stands until mastery of the whole of Unit 1 is demonstrated. You will demonstrate it here.”); the recovery board and blocked dial name the five-part obstacle; the four pupils, the practice cabinet, card, crank and bell, the AI's audit and the tally token are visible tools. Every gate had prose plus a physical label or plaque, plus in-world blocked feedback.

**Time to first meaningful insight.** From New Game (11:52) to Nia's chipped corner (11:56) took about four minutes in this session, which included testing four examine synonyms and three hint rungs. In ordinary play the card's chipped corner and Nia's pencil point at the insight within the first two or three commands. The final ward puzzle was understood when the recovery board's five labels were read and solved when the dial confirmed all five sockets lit.

**Repeated actions with no new information.** Four `examine register`-style synonyms at arrival correctly returned one in-world description, so those repeats were confirming rather than punishing. `examine recovery board` repeated the same five labels after each recovery; the dial block was repeated twice with the same accurate socket message; `hint` during cooldown repeated “Think about it for a moment.”; `talk-to priya` and `ask priya assumed` repeated her cheerful line. The one avoidable repetition was trying to retrieve the placed block after the stale Demonstration description (content finding 1).

**Guess-the-verb and clue access.** `PLACE` from the written card, `put`, both unlock word orders, the wrong-order refusal, Priya's own-words reason, the spaced and hyphenated `talk to` forms, the bell and the arrival nouns all worked with words the game itself offers. The only essential action that did not match its teaching was the exact-option-text quiz answer form (functional defect 1); `answer N` and bare `N` remained available, so no pupil was locked out.

**Hint level used.** Level 1 in each of the three ward rooms, level 2 and 3 in Intake when pressed, and the cooldown line on immediate repeats. Hints named the stalled object or half-step without supplying an answer, cost no score and were never offered automatically. After Nia's recovery, the next Intake rung pointed to the east route instead of repeating a solved task.

**Quiz readability and consequences.** Four questions were framed with numbered options; the three-of-four pass, zero score, no life loss, no movement and unlimited retries were stated before question 1. Every answer received a specific reason, including why each distractor was wrong. The deliberate 2/4 fail left the pupil in Records with three lives and an immediate retry; the retry passed 3/4. The unlock explanation named both working forms, so a correct answer could be copied into the room. A wrong answer changed the next attempt from guessing to using the explanation.

**Failure preservation and age-appropriate challenge.** No ward failure removed a record or a life, moved the pupil or forced a repeated walk. The reasoning involves observation, evidence, command form and a five-part sequence, which suits a GCSE learner; difficulty comes from interpretation rather than dense prose or punishment. A pupil could explain the dial failure (“ORIENTATION PASS needs the practice quiz”), the quiz outcome and Priya's two-half exchange, and then act on it. The two remaining friction points are the rejected taught answer form and the stale block description, not the ideas.

**After a hint or failure, could the player say what to try next and do it?** Yes for the dial, the quiz, Priya, Nia, the hub gates and each room hint. After the exact-option-text refusal the next step was clear only because `answer N` and bare `N` were also taught, so the defect did not cause abandonment.

**Abandonment point.** None in the ward. The point most likely to cause doubt was the repeated “I don't understand that.” after copying an option verbatim, resolved by switching to `answer 4` / a bare number.
